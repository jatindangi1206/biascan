Twelve articles were included in this research [17,36–46]. The characteristics of the reviewed studies and the main analysed variables are summarised in Table 2. Across all studies, a total of 570 individuals with multiple sclerosis (MS) and 478 control participants without a diagnosis of the disease were included, with 54% of the total study population corresponding to MS cases. Among the reported MS phenotypes, 53% (301/570) presented a relapsing–remitting course, 9.3% (53/570) were diagnosed with primary progressive MS (PPMS), and 3.5% (20/570) with benign MS (BMS). In 34.4% (196/570) of cases, the MS subtype was not specified, which limits the precision of phenotype-specific interpretations. Seven studies applied the McDonald 2010 diagnostic criteria, one used Poser criteria [44], and four did not specify the diagnostic framework. All studies reported demographic data. The women/men ratio was 388/182 (68%/32%) among MS cases and 394/184 (61.5%/38.5%) among controls. Only one study reported a higher proportion of men in both MS cases (60%) and controls (53%) [40]. Ethnicity was reported in two studies: one distinguished Caucasian, Hispanic, and Afro-American participants [38], while the other identified predominantly Caucasian participants, with 80% among controls and 95% among MS cases [17]. Geographically, three studies were conducted in the USA [17,38,39], two in Spain [36,45], and one each in Italy [37], Belgium [46], China [42], Brazil [44], Israel [41], Egypt [43], and Russia [40]. According to the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS), all included articles were assessed as having low risk of bias [35].
Gut Microbiome Composition and Variability
Ten of the twelve selected articles evaluated gut microbiota (GM), and approximately 80% analysed both alpha and beta diversity. Alpha diversity was assessed in eight studies, with heterogeneous findings. A decrease in alpha diversity was observed in relapsing–remitting MS (RRMS) cases in one study [39], whereas an increase was reported in PPMS in another [40]. The remaining studies did not identify statistically significant differences between MS cases and controls. Collectively, these results indicate variability in diversity measures across cohorts and disease phenotypes rather than a uniform directional change.
Taxonomic Diversity
Analysis of taxonomic composition across studies revealed inconsistent patterns (Table 4). At the phylum level, Firmicutes increased in 20% of the studies [36,38] but decreased in 40% [17,37,42,46]. Bacteroidetes increased in 30% [39,43,44] and decreased in 10% [17]. Actinobacteria showed both increases [36] and decreases [44], while Proteobacteria and Lentisphaerae decreased in 10% of the studies. These findings suggest that phylum-level shifts are not consistently reproducible across populations or methodologies.
At the class level, Clostridia increased in one study [38] and decreased in another [40]. At the family level, Lachnospiraceae decreased in controls in one study, whereas Ruminococcaceae both increased [40] and decreased in controls [44] in separate investigations. At the genus level, several taxa displayed divergent associations. Decreases in Bifidobacterium were reported in two studies [17,44], while reductions in Coprococcus [37,39], Butyricoccus [42,46], and Lachnospira [37,38] were also described. In contrast, Akkermansia was reported as increased relative to controls in three studies [37,38,40]. Blautia increased in three studies [36,42,44] but decreased in two others [37,38]. Similar bidirectional patterns were observed for Parabacteroides [17,37,44,46], Dorea [37,38,42], Ruminococcus, Faecalibacterium, Prevotella, Methanobrevibacter, and Dialister. Overall, genus-level findings were frequently context-dependent and varied by cohort characteristics, analytical pipelines, and clinical stratification.
Two studies examined microbiota differences across disease phases. Stratification by disease severity indicated lower diversity in secondary progressive MS (SPMS) compared with RRMS and controls in one cohort [37]. Another study compared microbiota composition across MS subtypes while considering interferon treatment, reporting lower richness in interferon-treated RRMS and in untreated RRMS during relapse, intermediate richness in controls and untreated active RRMS, and relatively higher richness in BMS and PPMS [46]. These observations suggest that treatment status and disease activity may influence microbial metrics, although the direction and magnitude of these effects were not consistent across all studies.
Across the ten studies evaluating gut microbiota, most reported some form of compositional difference between MS cases and controls; however, the specific taxa involved, the direction of change, and their potential clinical implications varied substantially by subtype, population, and study design [17,36–40,42–44,46]. Given the proportion of cases without subtype specification and the methodological heterogeneity, phenotype-specific conclusions remain tentative.
