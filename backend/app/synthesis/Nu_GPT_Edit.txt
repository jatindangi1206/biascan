The studies included in this systematic review were published between 2016 and 2022. Three studies (50%) were conducted in the USA, while the remaining three were conducted in Serbia (n=1, 16.7%), the UK (n=1, 16.7%), and the Netherlands (n=1, 16.7%). The sample size of the randomized controlled trials ranged from 12 to 66, for a total of 201 healthy older adults. The mean age of participants ranged from 67.0 ± 1.0 years to 76.0 ± 5.6 years. Yoshino et al. did not report gender distribution; therefore, gender-specific characterization is based on the remaining 181 participants (83 males and 98 females). Body mass index ranged from 25.3 ± 1.3 kg/m² to 28.6 ± 3.9 kg/m². Four studies did not report explicit standardization of diet or physical activity during the intervention, whereas Zanini et al. and Yoshino et al. instructed participants to maintain habitual dietary intake and activity levels. All included trials employed placebo control conditions (n=6, 100%) designed to be indistinguishable from the intervention products. Detailed study characteristics are reported in Table 1.
Nutraceutical intervention
Interventions differed substantially in compound type, dosage, and duration. The experimental treatments included sodium nitrite, fish oil-derived n-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA), hydrogen-rich water, nicotinamide riboside (NR), urolithin A, and a whey protein formulation containing L-tryptophan (L-Trp), nicotinic acid (NA), and nicotinamide (NAM).
– Sodium Nitrite. Sodium nitrite has been described as an intermediary in the nitrate–nitrite–nitric oxide pathway, with proposed influences on nitric oxide bioavailability and redox-related processes. In Rossman et al., participants received 80 mg/day (40 mg twice daily) for 12 weeks.
– Fish oil-derived n-3 PUFA. n-3 PUFA supplementation was motivated by preclinical observations of altered expression of genes associated with mitochondrial regulation and metabolic pathways. In Yoshino et al., participants received four 1-g capsules daily (LOVAZA®️), providing 1.86 g eicosapentaenoic acid and 1.50 g docosahexaenoic acid, for 6 months.
– Hydrogen-rich Water. Hydrogen-rich water has been proposed to interact with selected reactive species and to modulate oxidative and inflammatory pathways in experimental contexts. In Zanini et al., hydrogen-rich water was administered twice daily for 6 months.
– Nicotinamide Riboside. Nicotinamide riboside is a precursor of NAD⁺ that has demonstrated effects on mitochondrial bioenergetics in animal models. In Elhassan et al., participants received 250 mg capsules twice daily (total 1000 mg/day) for 21 days.
– Urolithin A. Urolithin A has been investigated for its capacity to influence mitochondrial-related gene expression and mitophagy in preclinical studies. In Liu et al., participants received 250 mg softgels four times daily (total 1000 mg/day) for 4 months.
– Whey Protein Powder (L-Trp, NA, NAM). L-tryptophan, nicotinic acid, and nicotinamide function as NAD⁺ precursors within cellular metabolic pathways. Their biological relevance to mitochondrial processes is commonly discussed in relation to NAD(H)-dependent redox reactions. In Connell et al., participants consumed a whey protein powder dissolved in 200 mL of orange juice once daily, providing niacin equivalents of 207.5 mg/day for 32 days.
Main findings in terms of muscle mitochondrial modifications
Outcomes were reported across a spectrum ranging from direct functional assessments to indirect molecular and transcriptomic markers.
(a) Functional measures of mitochondrial oxidative capacity.
Yoshino et al. evaluated mitochondrial-related pathways using microarray analyses and reported statistically significant within-group increases (p < 0.05) in gene sets associated with respiratory electron transport, oxidative phosphorylation, and related pathways following n-3 PUFA supplementation compared with baseline. These findings represent transcriptomic changes rather than direct physiological measurements of oxidative capacity. In contrast, Liu et al. assessed maximal ATP production in specific skeletal muscles and did not observe statistically significant between-group differences following urolithin A supplementation (p = NS). Elhassan et al., using high-resolution respirometry, reported no significant improvements in skeletal muscle oxidative phosphorylation or maximal respiratory capacity after NR supplementation (p = NS), and western blot analyses did not demonstrate significant alterations in protein acetylation. Similarly, Connell et al. found no significant between-group differences in ADP-stimulated or maximally uncoupled mitochondrial respiration measured ex vivo (p = 0.882 and p = 0.495, respectively).
(b) Mitochondrial antioxidant-related indicators.
Rossman et al. reported a statistically significant reduction (–35%, p < 0.05) in mitochondrial-specific reactive oxygen species bioactivity following sodium nitrite supplementation compared with placebo, suggesting a change in oxidative stress–related biomarkers rather than a direct measure of mitochondrial performance.
(c) Mitochondrial volume.
Using MitoTracker fluorescence, Rossman et al. did not identify statistically significant differences in mitochondrial volume after sodium nitrite supplementation (p = NS).
(d) Molecular and metabolic correlates of mitochondrial bioenergetics.
Liu et al. observed statistically significant reductions in plasma acylcarnitine and ceramide concentrations (p < 0.05), which are circulating biomarkers commonly interpreted in relation to mitochondrial and metabolic status. Elhassan et al. reported significant increases in metabolites associated with nicotinamide methylation pathways, including methyl-nicotinamide and related derivatives (p ≤ 0.006), indicating alterations in NAD⁺ metabolome profiles. Connell et al. similarly found higher skeletal muscle methyl-nicotinamide levels in the intervention group (p = 0.001). Zanini et al. did not observe significant changes in the NAD⁺/NADH ratio following hydrogen-rich water supplementation. Collectively, these outcomes reflect biochemical or metabolomic shifts that may be consistent with modified cellular redox or cofactor availability, without directly establishing changes in mitochondrial functional capacity.
(e) Transcriptomic and protein expression markers related to mitochondrial pathways.
Yoshino et al. reported statistically significant increases in the expression of UCP3 (~30%, p < 0.05) and UQCRC1 (~20%, p < 0.05) after n-3 PUFA supplementation compared with baseline, while no significant differences were observed for several additional mitochondrial-associated genes. These findings indicate selective changes in gene expression related to mitochondrial pathways without concurrent functional confirmation. Elhassan et al., through western blot analyses, did not detect statistically significant differences in the expression of selected mitochondrial proteins in skeletal muscle lysates (p = NS).
Overall, the evidence indicates heterogeneous effects across nutraceutical types and outcome categories, with several statistically significant molecular or metabolomic changes observed alongside a predominance of null findings in direct functional measurements of mitochondrial respiration or oxidative phosphorylation. The included trials were characterized by relatively short intervention durations, modest sample sizes, and enrollment of generally healthy older adults; accordingly, observed associations should be interpreted within these contextual limits and should not be assumed to represent long-term or clinical functional adaptations without further corroborating evidence.
