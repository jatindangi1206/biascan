Study Identification and Selection
Following PRISMA 2020 guidelines, studies were identified from the provided corpus of peer-reviewed observational and translational investigations examining the association between multiple sclerosis (MS) and the gut microbiome. After screening for relevance to MS populations and gut microbiota outcomes, a total of twelve studies met inclusion criteria. These comprised cross-sectional case–control studies, cohort analyses, and integrated microbiome–metabolome investigations conducted across diverse geographic populations, including Europe, the Middle East, Asia, Africa, and South America. No randomized controlled trials were identified. All included studies provided original empirical data comparing MS patients with healthy controls or between MS phenotypic subgroups.
Study Characteristics and Risk of Bias
The included studies collectively evaluated over 1,000 participants, encompassing relapsing–remitting MS (RRMS), primary progressive MS (PPMS), clinically isolated syndrome, and treatment-naïve early MS cohorts, alongside matched healthy controls. Most studies employed 16S rRNA gene sequencing of fecal samples, with variable hypervariable regions (V3–V4 or V4), while one large study applied shotgun metagenomics combined with serum metabolomics and host transcriptomics. Risk of bias was primarily related to cross-sectional design, limited sample sizes in subgroup analyses, and heterogeneity in sequencing platforms and bioinformatic pipelines. Several studies adjusted for confounders such as age, sex, diet, body mass index, and disease-modifying therapy exposure, while others reported unadjusted comparisons.
Overall Gut Microbiome Differences Between MS and Controls
Across geographically and ethnically diverse cohorts, MS was consistently associated with altered gut microbiome composition compared with healthy controls. Multiple studies reported reduced microbial richness or altered beta diversity in MS, although the direction and magnitude of alpha diversity changes varied by disease subtype and treatment status. Several taxa were repeatedly implicated, including increased relative abundance of Akkermansia muciniphila and Bacteroides species, alongside reduced abundance of Bifidobacterium, Faecalibacterium, and other short-chain fatty acid (SCFA)–producing Firmicutes.
Functional and Metabolomic Alterations
Beyond taxonomic shifts, functional differences in microbial metabolic capacity were observed. An integrated microbiome–metabolome study demonstrated that individuals with MS exhibited significant depletion of butyrate-producing bacterial species and genes involved in butyrate synthesis, despite similar circulating butyrate levels compared with controls. The same study identified significantly lower serum levels of microbiota-associated tryptophan metabolites, including indolelactate and indolepropionate, which discriminated MS cases from controls with high accuracy (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve ≈0.97). Altered circulating fatty acid profiles were also associated with gut microbiota dysbiosis and systemic inflammation in MS.
Disease Subtypes, Activity, and Disability
Microbiome variation differed across MS phenotypes. In RRMS, reduced microbial richness was reported during active disease or interferon treatment compared with benign or progressive forms. In contrast, a dedicated PPMS cohort study demonstrated increased overall bacterial diversity, driven mainly by differences in minor and rare taxa, including increased abundance of Gemmiger spp. and unclassified Ruminococcaceae. Associations between specific microbial taxa and clinical disability measures were reported, including inverse correlations between butyrate-producing bacteria and disease severity scores, as well as correlations between acetate levels and neurological disability.
Treatment Status and Ethnicity
Studies of treatment-naïve and early MS cohorts indicated that microbiome alterations are detectable near disease onset and are not solely attributable to disease-modifying therapies. Ethnicity-stratified analyses showed a consistent increase in Clostridium genus abundance across Caucasian, Hispanic, and African American MS patients, suggesting shared microbiome signatures despite population-level differences. However, heterogeneity across studies limited quantitative synthesis, and no formal meta-analysis was feasible.
Certainty of Evidence
Overall certainty of evidence was moderate for an association between MS and gut microbiome dysbiosis, supported by consistency across multiple independent cohorts and convergent taxonomic and functional findings. Certainty was low to moderate for specific taxa or metabolites due to between-study heterogeneity, cross-sectional designs, and limited longitudinal data. No study provided causal inference.
