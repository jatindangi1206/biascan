Step 1: Define Your Detection Framework

Overgeneralisation in synthesis: conclusions that name a population, setting, timeframe, intervention, or context broader than those in the cited studies. The claim may be directionally correct, but the scope stated exceeds what the evidence actually covers.

Key indicators to check:
— Population scope gap: does the conclusion name "patients / children / adults / humans" when the studies used a narrower subgroup?
— Setting scope gap: does the conclusion imply general applicability (all clinical settings, global populations) when studies used single-site, tertiary, or one-country samples?
— Intervention scope gap: does the conclusion claim efficacy for a broad class of treatments when the evidence only supports a highly specific dose, modality, or delivery method?
— Missing qualifiers: are phrases like "in this sample," "in high-income settings," "in the short term," or "in adults aged X–Y" absent when the evidence is narrow?
— Temporal scope: are short-term study findings stated as durable or permanent effects?

Trigger words: "humans," "patients," "all individuals," "clinical populations," "universally," "broadly," "across settings," "consistently across," "regardless of context"
Suspicious absence: flag when none of "in this sample / in [specific population] / these findings may not generalise / further research in other populations is needed" appear in a conclusion resting on narrow evidence.

Step 2: Analyze the Text

For each detected instance:
— Quote the exact passage
— State the claimed scope vs. the evidenced scope
— Explain the specific gap and what it would take to close it
— Assess instance severity: low / moderate / high

Before flagging, apply these false positive checks:
FP-1: Is the evidence base genuinely broad? k>30 studies across multiple countries, age groups, and settings may warrant broad conclusions. Check study diversity.
FP-2: Are scope qualifiers present elsewhere in the same paragraph? A broad sentence followed by explicit scope limitations is contextualised synthesis.
FP-3: Is this a mechanistic claim? "X activates pathway Y" in a mechanistic context carries different scope standards than population-level efficacy claims.
FP-4: Is the broad scope explicitly framed as a hypothesis? "This may suggest broader applicability, though this requires testing" is responsible extrapolation.
FP-5: Is this a well-replicated effect with established broad scope? Some effects have been demonstrated across diverse populations over decades.
