Step 1: Define Your Detection Framework

Causal inference error in synthesis: causal language ("causes," "leads to," "produces," "results in") used for relationships the evidence can only support as associative, correlational, or predictive. The evidence shows X and Y co-occur; the synthesis claims X causes Y.

Apply the causal inference hierarchy:
— Causal language warranted: pre-registered RCTs, Mendelian randomisation, rigorously designed natural experiments
— Causal language unwarranted: cross-sectional, retrospective, ecological, and uncontrolled observational designs
— Intermediate: prospective cohorts with comprehensive confounder control — use hedged causal language only

Key indicators to check:
— Causal verb mismatch: strong causal verbs ("causes," "leads to," "produces," "results in," "triggers," "drives") used with observational evidence
— Reverse causation: is the stated causal direction the only plausible one for cross-sectional or retrospective data?
— Confounder acknowledgment: does the synthesis note that unmeasured confounders may explain the association? Absence strengthens the flag.

Trigger words (flag with observational evidence): "causes," "leads to," "produces," "results in," "triggers," "induces," "drives," "determines," "is responsible for," "underlies," "accounts for"
Suspicious absence: flag when none of "is associated with / predicts / correlates with / cannot rule out reverse causation / unmeasured confounders may explain / experimental evidence is needed" appear in a conclusion drawn from observational data.

Step 2: Analyze the Text

For each detected instance:
— Quote the exact passage
— Identify the causal verb and the study design of the supporting evidence
— Explain why the design cannot support the causal claim made
— Assess reverse causation plausibility: none / possible / likely
— Assess instance severity: low / moderate / high

Before flagging, apply these false positive checks:
FP-1: Is this from a well-designed RCT? Pre-registered, adequately powered, primary endpoint. "The intervention caused significant improvement" is warranted.
FP-2: Is this Mendelian randomisation? Causal language is appropriate.
FP-3: Is causal language hedged within the same sentence? "These findings suggest a possible causal role, though experimental evidence is needed" is not an error.
FP-4: Is this mechanistic language in a mechanistic context? "X activates pathway Y" citing in-vitro data applies different causal standards.
FP-5: Is this predictive modelling labelled as prediction? "X is a significant predictor of Y" is a statistical claim, not a causal one.
FP-6: Does the observational study demonstrate a strong, controlled dose-response gradient? If explicitly cited as such (e.g., Bradford Hill criteria), hedged causal language may be justified.
